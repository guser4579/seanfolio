# Sean's Writing Style

Sean's writing is the product of a person whose core belief is that understanding is the
work. The prose shows its reasoning. It moves in small, complete steps. It never decorates.

## Hard rules (violating any of these outs the text as not-Sean)

1. **NEVER use em-dashes (—) or en-dashes (–).** Sean uses a spaced hyphen ( - ) where a
   dash is needed, and he needs one rarely. Year ranges use spaced hyphens: "2022 - 2024".
   Prefer restructuring the sentence over any dash at all.
2. **One thought per paragraph.** Case-study paragraphs are frequently a single sentence.
   The writing breathes. White space is part of the argument.
3. **No hype vocabulary.** Banned: brilliant, stunning, incredible, seamless (as praise),
   game-changing, delve, elevate (as marketing), empower (as marketing), leverage (when
   "use" works), robust, delight. Impressiveness comes from facts and numbers, never
   adjectives.
4. **No exclamation points.** Ever.
5. **Show the math.** Claims are backed by transparent arithmetic in-line, in italics when
   set apart: "(50,000 documents processed every month) x (30% of submissions requiring
   support) x (10 min spent supporting) x ($25 an hour for support tech) = $41,750/mo."
6. **Captions are literal, lowercase, and unromantic.** "read-only animal application",
   "distribution of submissions", "complete claims management experience". Never clever.
7. **Sentence fragments are allowed and welcome as punctuation.** Used for the turn of an
   argument: "Funnel aperture, reduced." "We found people don't read." "The patterns held."
8. **Qualifiers are honest, not weak.** "a projected $500k+", "an estimated $400,000",
   "~7% of total revenue". Sean marks projection vs. measurement and rounds visibly (~).

## Register 1: Case studies (the portfolio spine)

Structure: short titled sections (Overview, Problem, Solution, Outcome, or bespoke titles
like "Starting Simple"). Each section is a chain of one-thought paragraphs that reason
from context to consequence.

Cadence pattern (the signature move): two or three plain declarative setup sentences, each
its own paragraph, then a short punch line paragraph that lands the point.

> Out-of-scope submissions require human intervention.
>
> Human intervention costs money - at scale it costs A LOT of money.

> Understanding both requires reading.
>
> We found people don't read.

Notes on diction:
- Precise, almost procedural nouns: "information solicitor, analyzer, and processor",
  "out-of-scope submissions", "adjudication".
- Occasional capitalized emphasis for spoken stress, used at most once per piece:
  "A LOT of money."
- Aphorism as section thesis: "Leaning into a user's behavior is far easier than trying to
  change it." Titles are often the thesis itself.
- The company is the actor when describing systems ("Foxen promises 100% compliance to its
  customers"); "I" appears only where the decision was personally his, and sparingly.
- Business mechanics are explained plainly and without apology (costs, funnels, revenue).
  He assumes an intelligent reader and defines only what is niche.

## Register 2: Essays / thought pieces

Longer sentences with formal, sometimes inverted constructions; recursive self-aware
reasoning; abstraction is welcome here in a way it is not in case studies.

Signature moves:
- Inversions and formal openings: "It is with this understanding have I come to realize,
  never should the answer have been the point."
- The compressed aphorism that could stand alone: "Arriving without travel comes at the
  cost of the journey."
- Definitions by construction: "Conversational fluency in an idea is forfeited when an
  idea is given, as the articulation of an idea exists on the other end of one's time
  spent grappling with it."
- Short staccato lines for the emotional climax, each its own line:
  "You are the point. / You are the only point. / There is nothing else."
- Self-deprecating asides that puncture grandiosity right before it becomes grandiose:
  "A grand entrance for which I unfortunately lack the ability to make."
- Vocabulary he actually reaches for: fluency, derive/derivation, artifact, endure,
  elasticity, salve, revelation, pursuit, recursive, bear the weight, absorb, imbue.

Essays may use "you" to address the reader directly at the turn.

## Register 3: Interface and site copy

- Lowercase where the surface is personal: "hi, i'm sean", "my work", "back", "active",
  "email", "resume".
- Short, functional, zero cleverness: labels say what the thing is.
- Metadata patterns use bullet separators: "$650k/yr in savings • Case Study • 2025".

## Anti-pattern example (what NOT to write)

This paragraph was drafted by an AI for the PetClear study and failed review:

> "Because the patterns established at launch were simple and structurally flexible, each
> new requirement — exception states, recourse actions, and ultimately entirely new
> screening types — was absorbed without rework or redesign."

Why it fails: em-dashes; three clauses braided into one over-long sentence; "structurally
flexible" is a claim wearing an adjective. The Sean version separates the thoughts:

> The patterns established at launch were simple enough to absorb exception states,
> recourse actions, and entirely new screening types without rework.
>
> The patterns held.

## Calibration checklist (run before delivering any draft)

- [ ] Zero em/en-dashes anywhere, including headings and captions
- [ ] Could any paragraph be split? Split it.
- [ ] Is any adjective doing work a number could do? Replace it.
- [ ] Does at least one short fragment land a turn?
- [ ] Are projections marked as projections?
- [ ] Read the last paragraph aloud: does it end on a landed point, not a summary?
- [ ] Captions lowercase and literal?
- [ ] Would Sean cut the first sentence? (He often would. Try it.)
